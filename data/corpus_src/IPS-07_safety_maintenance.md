# IPS-07 Electrical Safety and Maintenance Practices

## 1. Hazard overview

The main hazards of industrial electrical systems are electric shock, arc flash and arc blast, and fire. Arc flash is the release of thermal energy from an electric arc between conductors or from a conductor to ground; it can cause severe burns at a distance of several metres and the pressure wave can throw workers and debris. The incident energy, expressed in cal/cm2, depends mainly on available fault current, arcing time and working distance.

## 2. Establishing an electrically safe work condition

Work on de-energised equipment requires an electrically safe work condition. The general sequence is: identify all sources of supply from up-to-date drawings; interrupt the load current and open the disconnecting devices; visually verify open contacts where possible; apply lockout/tagout (LOTO) devices so that each worker has personal control; test for absence of voltage with a properly rated tester that is verified on a known live source before and after the test; and apply temporary protective grounding where induced voltage or stored energy could be present. Capacitors and cables can retain dangerous charge after disconnection and must be discharged.

## 3. Arc flash risk assessment

An arc flash risk assessment determines whether an arc flash hazard exists, estimates the incident energy at the working distance, defines the arc flash boundary, and selects personal protective equipment (PPE) with an arc rating at least equal to the estimated incident energy. The arc flash boundary is the distance at which incident energy falls to 1.2 cal/cm2, the level associated with the onset of a second-degree burn on bare skin. Reducing the clearing time of protection, for example with maintenance switches or differential protection, is often the most effective way to reduce incident energy.

## 4. Preventive maintenance and testing

Insulation resistance testing applies a DC test voltage, commonly 500 V or 1000 V for low-voltage equipment, and measures leakage resistance; trending results over time is more informative than any single value. The polarization index is the ratio of the 10-minute to the 1-minute insulation resistance reading; a low value can indicate moisture or contamination. Infrared thermography under load identifies hot spots at loose or corroded connections; a temperature rise of several tens of degrees above similar components under the same load usually calls for prompt corrective action. Protective relays and circuit breakers should be tested periodically by secondary or primary injection to confirm that they operate within their settings.

## 5. Protective devices and coordination

Overcurrent protection is coordinated so that the device closest to a fault operates first, minimising the extent of the outage; this is called selectivity or discrimination. Time-current curves of upstream and downstream devices are compared to confirm adequate margins. Ground-fault protection, differential protection for transformers and generators, and under-frequency relays for load shedding (see IPS-05) are other common protection functions in industrial plants.

## 6. Documentation

Single-line diagrams, protective device settings, short-circuit and arc flash study results, and equipment labels must be kept up to date. Changes to the system, such as a larger transformer or new motor loads, can change fault levels and invalidate earlier studies.
